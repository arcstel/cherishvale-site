Discuss a pilot Why it matters

What it is

Hearthlight is an acoustic detection node for schools and other facilities. Four microphones, a Raspberry Pi-class computer, and no lens. It listens for the shape of a muzzle blast, and when it hears one it sends a short alert — room, time, confidence, direction — into whatever system you already run.

The whole product is shaped by one constraint: it has to be accepted, in writing, by a district's technology, safety, legal, and community groups. That constraint decides what is in the box and, more importantly, what is not.

Why this matters

In severe trauma the outcome is largely settled long before anyone reaches an operating room. It is settled by how early the response starts — and a response cannot start until someone knows.

Minutes are the whole game

Survival in severe trauma falls with every minute of delayed care. That is established medicine, not a product claim. It is the entire reason this device exists, and it is why the design refuses every step that could add one: no cloud round-trip, no human who has to notice first, no dependency on anyone reaching a phone.

How it gets reported today

Someone has to see it. Find a phone. Stay calm enough to speak. Describe where they are in a building they may not know. Every one of those steps is a person under stress, in the dark, and every one of them spends minutes nobody has.

What the mechanism changes

The node is already listening. It needs no one to notice anything and no one to reach a phone — it fires on the sound itself, and it reports a room, so the first minutes go toward the problem instead of into establishing where it is. And when the network has been cut, detection and the local alarm still work, because neither one ever leaves the device.

What we will not put on this page.

A detection rate, a response-time improvement, or any other figure we have not measured in a real building. Everything above is how the system works and why it is built that way — mechanism, not marketing. How well it performs is exactly what a pilot exists to find out, and those numbers will be published when we have them.

How it works

Listen

Stage 1 runs continuously and cheaply, asking only one question: did a sharp, fast-rising event just happen? Onset speed is the separator. A muzzle blast reaches peak in well under a millisecond; a door slam, a dropped book, or a body fall ramps over several. Energy alone is why naive detectors cry wolf in a school.

Decide

Only when Stage 1 fires does Stage 2 run: twenty-two measurable features and a small decision tree. Not a neural embedding — a safety director will ask why the system called a shot, and the answer has to be a human-readable reason, not a number.

Alert

A few hundred bytes: node, room, timestamp, confidence, shot count, direction. Outbound only, to the platform you already use. Plus a local buzzer on the wall that keeps working when the network does not.

Precision over recall — deliberately.

A missed detection is a failure. A false lockdown in an occupied school is worse: it makes the local news and ends the pilot. Hearthlight is tuned to be reasonably sure before it speaks, and its calibration step will refuse to deploy an operating point it cannot statistically defend. We would rather tell you it stayed quiet than invent a detection rate.

Privacy, by construction

Raw audio is captured, processed in memory, and discarded. It never leaves the device.

No recording

There is no buffer, no clip attached to an alert, no "keep the last ten seconds for debugging," and no audio upload path. Not disabled by default — absent from the design.

No camera

No lens, no image sensor, no imaging hardware of any kind. The box has an acoustic port and one status light. It is built to read as infrastructure, like a smoke detector, not as surveillance.

Room-level, not tracking

The node reports a bearing — "north corridor" — not a person's path through a building. Precise tracking invites a surveillance conversation nobody wants, and a room is what a lockdown decision needs.

No inbound connections

The node accepts no inbound connection. Outbound HTTPS to one destination, and nothing else. A device that cannot be connected to cannot be an attack surface.

Metadata-only alerts

An alert is roughly 200 bytes of JSON. No audio, no identifiers, no student data. Keeping it that way is what keeps it outside FERPA.

Works air-gapped

Detection and the local buzzer are entirely on-device. A district with no route to the internet still gets a working alarm, and alerts queue with their true timestamps until connectivity returns.

These are design commitments, and the same promises in writing are in our one-page attestation, which we send to your counsel before a pilot begins.

How a pilot runs

Mount, buzzer only

One building, ceiling corners, local alarm. Nothing connected to mass notification yet.

Log a full term

Every trigger and every decision — counts and confidence, never audio. The number that matters is how often it was wrong.

Tune against the real log

Calibrate the threshold against that building's actual acoustic environment, not a laboratory's.

Then go live

Only after the false-alarm rate is understood does the node talk to your mass-notification system.

The full structure of a pilot — what a district provides, what it gets back, and the grant pathways that typically fund school-safety technology — is on the pilot program page. Status: in development, seeking pilots.

Hearthlight is not a shipped product. The detection core is built and tested; what it needs is real buildings, real ambient noise, and real pilots before any accuracy figure means anything. We publish no performance numbers today, and we will only publish numbers measured on recordings we collected in the building types we name.

Questions

Does it record audio?

No. Sound is captured, processed in memory, and discarded immediately after classification. There is no rolling buffer and no audio in the alert payload. If you need to verify what the node hears, the answer is a count and a confidence score, not a playback.

Is there a camera anywhere in it?

No. There is no lens and no image sensor. This is a deliberate design constraint: a lens changes what the object is, legally and in the eyes of a school community, and a device that looks like surveillance gets rejected regardless of how well it works.

What exactly is in an alert?

Roughly 200 bytes of JSON: node identifier, room, UTC timestamp, confidence, number of shots, and a bearing if the microphone array can resolve one. No audio, no identifiers of people, no student data.

What is the false alarm rate?

Not yet published, because we have not measured it in a real building — and that is the only measurement that counts. Numbers produced from simulated audio are an engineering check, not a performance claim. The first term of a pilot exists precisely to establish this figure, and we will publish it once we have it.

What happens if the network fails?

Detection and the local buzzer are entirely on the device, so the room alarm still fires. Delivery to your notification platform is best-effort with retries; queued alerts carry their true timestamps, so the sequence is correct when connectivity returns.

What hardware is it?

A Raspberry Pi-class single-board computer, a four-microphone I2S array on a fixed carrier board, a sealed tamper-evident enclosure with an acoustic port, and a local buzzer. Roughly the footprint and appearance of a smoke detector. Power over Ethernet or a battery backup.

Will it work in our building?

Acoustics vary enormously — a carpeted library and a hard-surfaced corridor are different problems, and a locker slam is a harder problem than a gunshot. That is exactly why we are looking for pilots rather than shipping a box with a number on the carton. One building type, one full term, honest logging.
