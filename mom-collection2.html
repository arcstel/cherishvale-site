Likely dateMid-to-late 20th century; decorative rather than antique
Material / originGlazed earthenware or stoneware; apple-green glaze with deliberate crazing; origin unknown
Preliminary resale range$15–$50
ConfidenceObject type: medium · date/origin: low

Squat, wide-mouthed jar with a short collar and a dense green crackle glaze over a cream body. The crazing (fine crackle in the glaze) appears intentional—a decorative effect used on many 20th-century wares imitating older Chinese Ge or celadon styles—rather than damage. Photograph the base for any mark or “Made in” stamp; measure height and diameter; check the interior for water lines, residue, or repair; note whether a lid exists elsewhere.

21. Pair of blue frosted-glass mermaid candlesticks

Likely date1930s if genuine Walther & Söhne; later reproductions also exist
Probable makerAuguste Walther & Söhne (Germany), “Nymphen” pattern, catalogued 1934
Preliminary resale range$80–$200 the pair if period; $20–$60 if later reproduction
ConfidencePattern match: medium-high · authenticity: low without marks

This pair closely matches the Art Deco “Nymphen” (mermaid/siren) candlesticks produced by the German glassworks Auguste Walther & Söhne of Ulm, featured in their 1934 catalogue: frosted cobalt-blue glass, a mermaid figure winding up the stem beneath a flower-shaped candle cup, on a square foot. Genuine examples typically stand about 8.5 inches tall. The design was popular and has been reproduced, so the match to the pattern does not by itself prove age. Look for ground pontil marks, glass quality, wear on the bases, and any acid stamp or label.

Verification to request

Photograph the underside of each base (look for pontil scarring, etched marks, or labels); measure height; check for chips at the petal rims and mermaid tails; hold to light for bubbles and consistency.

Reference: 20th Century Glass — Walther & Söhne “Nymphen” mermaid candlesticks (sold/reference listing describing the 1934 catalogue pattern).

Preliminary resale range$50–$200; higher if a known period or signed studio piece

Large double-gourd (hulu) form—symbolic of good fortune in Chinese culture—decorated with a continuous mountain-and-pine landscape including a yellow-roofed pavilion, painted in green, blue, iron-red, and yellow enamels over a white glaze. Photograph the base and any seal or reign mark; measure height; inspect for rim flakes, enamel wear, and repairs; check whether the piece is hand-painted or decal-decorated by looking closely at brush strokes.

23. Blue-and-white transferware courting-couple pitcher

Likely datePattern dates to the 19th century; this example may be a later (20th-century) reproduction
Material / originUnderglaze-blue transfer-printed earthenware; English Staffordshire type likely
Preliminary resale range$20–$60 as a likely later piece; $80–$250 if a documented early-19th-century example
ConfidenceObject type: high · date/authenticity: low

Globular pitcher with a blue transfer-printed scene of a courting couple beneath a spreading tree, in the English pastoral tradition that flourishes from the 1830s onward (often grouped loosely under “Willow”-style romantic patterns). The blue handle and spout treatment are typical of flow-blue and related wares. Many such pitchers were reproduced through the 20th century for the souvenir and decorative market. The base mark, engraving clarity, blue tone, and potting thickness are the keys to separating a period piece from a later copy.

Verification to request

Photograph the underside for a backstamp (maker, pattern name, “England,” or a registration number); measure height; note weight and whether the blue is crisp (early) or slightly blurred/flowing; check the rim and handle for chips.

Context: Winterthur primer on English underglaze-blue transferware · Skinner lot of early-19th-century blue transferware tableware.

24. Brass lamp with milk-glass melon shade

Likely dateEarly-to-mid 20th century; converted oil lamp or purpose-built electric table lamp
MaterialBrass base with burner-style hardware; white milk-glass (opal) ribbed “melon” shade
Preliminary resale range$30–$80 as a decorative lamp; more if rewired by a professional and confirmed period
ConfidenceObject type: medium · date: low

Table lamp with a brass font-style base still showing burner hardware and a wick-adjuster lever, topped by a large white milk-glass shade in a ribbed melon or pumpkin form with a flared fitter rim. This style was common both as original early-20th-century electric lighting and as converted kerosene/oil lamps. Verification to request

Photograph the underside of the base for maker marks or felt-pad removal; inspect the cord, plug, and socket for age and fraying; check the shade for chips at the fitter rim; measure shade diameter and overall height. Have a lamp technician evaluate before use.

26. Ornate silver-plated teapot

Likely dateVictorian-revival styling; likely late 19th to mid-20th century
MaterialSilverplate is most likely; sterling is unverified and less likely from appearance
Preliminary resale range$30–$100 if silverplate; more if sterling or a named maker
ConfidenceObject type: medium · metal content: low

Baluster-form teapot with a domed lid, scrolled handle with thumbpiece, curved spout, and four cast cabriole feet—all in a Rococo-revival taste popular in Victorian silverplate and its 20th-century revivals. Heavy tarnish covers the surface; this is normal and should not be aggressively polished until the piece is identified, as polishing can reduce collector appeal and hide maker’s marks. The feet and handle joins are typical places to find hallmarks or maker’s stamps.

Verification to request

Photograph the underside, the base of the handle, and the underside of the lid for “STERLING,” “925,” “EPNS,” “A1,” “plate,” or a maker’s mark; measure height and capacity; weigh it. A magnet test helps: sterling is non-magnetic, while many plated bases are too, so a mark check is still required.

Market context: Unmarked silverplate holloware typically sells in the low hundreds or less at retail; marked sterling pieces follow silver weight plus collector premium. See the silverplate tray comparables in batch 1, item 4 for related market range.

28. Blue-and-white “hundred boys at play” charger

Likely dateEarly-to-mid 20th century export; the seal mark is likely apocryphal, not a period reign mark
Material / originPorcelain with underglaze-blue decoration; Chinese export, painted in the traditional “hundred boys” (baizi tu) motif
Preliminary resale range$80–$250 as a likely 20th-century export piece; substantially more only if authenticated as an early (18th–19th-century) example
ConfidenceMotif/object type: high · date/authenticity: low-medium

Large blue-and-white charger depicting a crowded scene of boys at play—a classic Chinese “hundred boys” (百子, baizi) motif symbolizing many sons and continued lineage—with a lion-dance performance at the center, scholars’ rock and architectural vignettes around the well, and a scrolling foliate/floral border on the rim. This subject was popular from the Ming and Qing dynasties onward and remained a staple of export porcelain into the 20th century. The painting style, blue tone, and potting seen here are consistent with early-to-mid-20th-century export production rather than a Kangxi- or Qianlong-period original, though only close inspection of the paste, glaze, and brushwork can settle that. The underside carries a six-character double-column seal mark in archaic script (see photo 170227), the kind of reign-style mark frequently copied apocryphally on later export wares.

Verification to request

Photograph the full base including the foot rim and mark in sharp focus; measure the diameter; check the rim and back for chips, hairlines, or repair; examine the blue under magnification for the stippling and stroke character of hand-painting versus transfer printing; compare the paste color and weight to known period examples. Have a Chinese ceramics specialist read the seal mark and assess whether it is a genuine reign mark, a workshop mark, or an apocryphal copy.

Comparables: Early-20th-century blue-and-white boys charger with Kangxi mark, estimate $400–$600, realized $250 · 19th-century boys charger, hammer £340 · Early-20th-century boys platter, retail $195.

ObjectUnderside of the same charger as item 28
Mark visibleSix-character double-column mark in seal script, underglaze blue
InterpretationReign-style or workshop mark; commonly apocryphal on 20th-century export pieces
Preliminary resale rangeDoes not by itself raise the $80–$250 estimate for item 28

The mark is painted in the same underglaze blue as the decoration, arranged in two vertical columns of three characters in archaic seal script (zhuanshu). Marks in this format on blue-and-white export chargers frequently imitate Qing reign marks—especially Kangxi (康熙) and Qianlong (乾隆)—long after those periods, as a sign of style rather than a claim of date. A specialist reading of the characters, combined with examination of the paste and glaze, is required before treating this as anything more than a decorative homage. Refer to the complete charger record.

Verification to request

Re-photograph the mark straight-on in bright, even light so the characters are legible; note whether it sits inside a double circle or square frame; check the foot rim for wear consistent with age; compare the blue of the mark to the blue of the scene (they should match if original to the piece).
